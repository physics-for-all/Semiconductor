01 — FUNDAMENTALS

What is a semiconductor?

Its electrical conductivity lies between that of a conductor and an insulator, but its most important feature is that conductivity can be deliberately controlled.

A semiconductor is a material whose electrical properties arise from the behaviour of electrons and holes within its electronic energy bands. Common examples include silicon (Si), germanium (Ge) and gallium arsenide (GaAs).

Unlike metals, semiconductors generally have a finite energy gap between the valence band and the conduction band. Thermal energy, incident photons or electrical excitation can provide the energy needed to generate mobile charge carriers.

Their conductivity can be adjusted by changing temperature, introducing controlled impurities called dopants, applying an electric field or illuminating the material. These mechanisms are the foundation of semiconductor devices.

“The key is not simply conducting electricity, but controlling how charge moves through a material.” THE CENTRAL IDEA OF SEMICONDUCTOR PHYSICS
Electrical behaviour / Comparison

Conductors

Many mobile charge carriers; bands may overlap or be partially occupied. Examples: copper and aluminium.

Semiconductors

A moderate band gap allows carrier concentration to be engineered. Examples: silicon and gallium arsenide.

Insulators

Typically have a large band gap and very low electrical conductivity under ordinary conditions. Examples: glass and many ceramics.

02 — ELECTRONIC STRUCTURE

Understanding the band gap.

In solids, closely spaced atomic energy levels form bands. The separation between occupied and mobile electronic states helps determine how a material conducts electricity and interacts with light.

Conduction and valence bands

A simplified representation of semiconductor energy levels.

Eg = Ec − Ev

Eg is the band-gap energy. An electron must acquire sufficient energy to move from the valence band to the conduction band, leaving behind a hole.

Why the gap matters

The band gap influences electrical and optical behaviour.

Conductors can have available electronic states at nearby energies. Semiconductors have a moderate gap, while many insulators have a substantially larger gap. These are simplified schematic diagrams.

04 — CONTROL THROUGH DOPING

Engineering the charge carriers.

Doping introduces small, controlled concentrations of impurity atoms into a semiconductor. The dopants change the carrier concentration without changing the basic identity of the host material.

Type 01 / Donor doping

n-type semiconductor

Donor impurities provide electrons that can be excited into conducting states. In silicon, phosphorus is a common donor.

Majority carriers: electrons
Example: silicon doped with phosphorus.
Type 02 / Acceptor doping

p-type semiconductor

Acceptor impurities create available states that facilitate hole formation. In silicon, boron is a common acceptor.

Majority carriers: holes
Example: silicon doped with boron.

What happens when a semiconductor is heated?

Thermal excitation creates electron–hole pairs. In an intrinsic semiconductor, the carrier concentration rises strongly with temperature, often dominating the effect of reduced carrier mobility.

Where semiconductors make a difference.

By controlling the flow of charge and the interaction of light with matter, semiconductor devices underpin energy, communication, computing and measurement.

Solar cells

Photons generate electron–hole pairs. A built-in electric field separates charge carriers, producing electrical power from sunlight.

QUESTIONS & ANSWERS

Semiconductor essentials.

Concise explanations of the concepts most frequently encountered when studying semiconductor physics.

Why is silicon widely used in electronics?
Silicon is abundant, has a useful band gap, and forms a stable native oxide (SiO₂). Its mature manufacturing processes make it particularly suitable for integrated circuits and many photovoltaic devices.
What is the difference between electrons and holes?
An electron is a negatively charged carrier. A hole is the effective positive charge associated with a missing electron in an otherwise occupied electronic state. Both can contribute to electrical current.
Why does semiconductor conductivity often increase with temperature?
Higher temperature can generate more electron–hole pairs. In intrinsic semiconductors, this increase in carrier concentration often outweighs the reduction in mobility caused by increased lattice scattering. The behaviour of a doped semiconductor depends on its temperature regime and doping level.
What is a p–n junction?
It is the interface between p-type and n-type semiconductor regions. Carrier diffusion and recombination establish a depletion region and built-in electric potential. The junction's response to applied voltage is central to diodes, solar cells and many other devices.
How does a semiconductor solar cell generate electricity?
Absorbed photons with sufficient energy create electron–hole pairs. The device structure separates the carriers, and contacts collect them to deliver current to an external circuit. Real performance depends on absorption, recombination, transport and contact losses.
What is the difference between direct and indirect band gaps?
In a direct-gap semiconductor, the conduction-band minimum and valence-band maximum occur at the same crystal momentum. Radiative transitions can therefore occur efficiently without a phonon. In an indirect-gap semiconductor, momentum conservation generally requires phonon participation, making light emission less efficient.
