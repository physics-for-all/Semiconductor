01 — FUNDAMENTALS

What is a semiconductor?

Its electrical conductivity lies between that of a conductor and an insulator, but its most important feature is that conductivity can be deliberately controlled.

A semiconductor is a material whose electrical properties arise from the behaviour of electrons and holes within its electronic energy bands. Common examples include silicon (Si), germanium (Ge) and gallium arsenide (GaAs).

Unlike metals, semiconductors generally have a finite energy gap between the valence band and the conduction band. Thermal energy, incident photons or electrical excitation can provide the energy needed to generate mobile charge carriers.

Their conductivity can be adjusted by changing temperature, introducing controlled impurities called dopants, applying an electric field or illuminating the material. These mechanisms are the foundation of semiconductor devices.

“The key is not simply conducting electricity, but controlling how charge moves through a material.” THE CENTRAL IDEA OF SEMICONDUCTOR PHYSICS
Electrical behaviour / Comparison

Conductors

Many mobile charge carriers; bands may overlap or be partially occupied. Examples: copper and aluminium.

Semiconductors

A moderate band gap allows carrier concentration to be engineered. Examples: silicon and gallium arsenide.

Insulators

Typically have a large band gap and very low electrical conductivity under ordinary conditions. Examples: glass and many ceramics.

Understanding the band gap.

In solids, closely spaced atomic energy levels form bands. The separation between occupied and mobile electronic states helps determine how a material conducts electricity and interacts with light.
